    Two ways of writing

    • Blocks and rows is the fluent form. A native stem is packed into one square block. Particles, endings and function words follow in small glyphs, and loanwords are written in a row, the way Japanese writes foreign words in katakana. A compound is two blocks touching.
    • Row only is the learner's form. Every glyph is full size, one sound after another, spelled exactly like the Latin spelling. Both are correct Lunaji.

    Typing

    • Nasal vowels: type ã ĩ õ, or a~ i~ o~.
    • Front rounded vowels: type ü ö, or u: o:.
    • Inside a native stem, u before an i-syllable is spelled ü (R13). The app flags an old spelling like uwiki; suffixes, compound seams and loans are exempt.
    • - splits a stem from its endings yourself: lõji-wa. Use it when the app guesses the stem wrong, or to stop a split.
    • + joins compound blocks: koime+zaĩko.
    • * in front of a word writes it as a loan: *jura.
    • Numbers: type the digits, 27. A counter joins on: 3re 4ko. The ten digits are separate signs modelled on the Japanese numerals; zero is the empty cup. In Blocks and rows a number packs into a block; put a space in a long number (250 000) to split it into two blocks.
    • Quotation marks, straight or curly, become corner brackets 「 」. ? and ! have their own marks.
    • Elision is spoken, not written: d'itsuze is written as the whole particle di, then itsuze.

    How the app finds a stem

    It takes common particles and endings (ba, ni, pe, wa, di, na, -ze, -ri, -ta, -jo) off the end of a word, but only while at least two syllables remain and the word is not on the whole-stems list. Stand-alone function words like hena and mi are always written in the small glyphs.

    The letters

    • Consonant families follow the mouth: the lips share an open frame, the tongue tip a central vertical, the palate a cliff stroke, the back of the mouth a Z-shaped turn. A nasal consonant adds one horizontal. Voicing is the two ticks Japanese uses.
    • Vowels: none for u, a sweep on the right for a, a vertical on the left for i, two short bars for e, a left sweep for o. Rounding closes a front vowel's stroke: ü is the i vertical with a bar along its foot, ö the two e bars joined on the left. A nasal vowel adds a long sweep along the foot.
    • Glides and clusters are a stroke over the letter: a dot for y, two dots for w, a short sweep for r, a short bar for l. When a cluster and a glide come together (prya), both strokes sit over the letter, the cluster on the left.
    • A coda ends with a heavy stop dot. A long consonant is written as a coda of the same consonant followed by the syllable, like Japanese small tsu.

    Practising

    Read it shows a glyph and asks for its sound; Write it shows the sound, you write it on paper, then reveal it with its stroke order and mark yourself. Pick a letter family to learn one at a time. Anything you miss comes back more often, and the review list is kept on this device. Sheets makes printable practice pages: a model with stroke numbers, faint copies to trace, then empty squares.

    Stroke order

    Tap any glyph on the Chart or Write tab to see it written stroke by stroke, with numbers, a Play button and a strip of frames. Consonant first, with the moon curl flowing into its first stroke; then the glide or cluster, the vowel, the nasal foot, the voicing ticks, and last the coda dot. With a pen or pencil the taper disappears, and that is fine: the letter is its skeleton, not its brush shape.
